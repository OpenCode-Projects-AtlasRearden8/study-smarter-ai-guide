Chapter 5 — Citation Honesty

Study Smarter with AI

The principle: AI is a study partner, not a ghostwriter

Different schools draw the AI line in different places — some tools are banned outright on some assignments, others are welcome. So step zero is always the same: know your class's actual rules. Ask your instructor; it's a five-minute conversation that protects your whole semester.

But underneath every rulebook is one principle that protects you everywhere: anything with your name on it must represent your work. AI can help you get there — brainstorm, quiz you, explain, critique your draft — and the line between "help" and "do it for me" is clearer than it looks.

Green light / red light

Generally welcome (green light):

  • Quizzing you from your notes (Chapters 1 and 3)
  • Explaining a concept that confused you — after you tried the textbook
  • Asking you to explain it back and critiquing your explanation (Chapter 2)
  • Brainstorming angles for an essay you'll write yourself
  • Critiquing a draft you wrote: "what would a grader flag here?"
  • Checking your math steps, grammar, or citations format
  • Practice transcripts — like rehearsing nurse-to-patient explanations

Do this only with explicit permission (check your syllabus):

  • Polishing large sections of your writing
  • Summarizing a reading you're assigned to summarize yourself
  • Generating practice problems the instructor already provides

Never okay without permission (red light):

  • Submitting AI-written text as your own
  • "Cleaning up" your essay so heavily it's no longer your voice
  • Making up "sources" — even if AI also made them up, that's doubly dishonest

When in doubt, a tiny bit of transparency usually costs you nothing: "I used AI to quiz myself before writing this" is a sentence almost no instructor dislikes.

If you're allowed to use AI and must cite it

Some instructors permit AI use with a disclosure. Common practice (which usually beats guessing at a "correct" format): say which tool, which version if you know it, what you asked, and what part of your work it touched. Example of a disclosure paragraph:

I used [AI tool] to generate practice questions from my class notes and to critique a draft of Section 2. The analysis, argument, and final text of this work are my own.

If your course gives you a required citation format for AI, use that — formats change fast, and your instructor's word is the format that matters.

Three traps worth naming

  1. The invisible escalator. "Fix my grammar" → "make this sentence better" → "rewrite this paragraph" → you're sliding toward submitting not-your-work. Notice the slope. Each small step feels harmless; the destination is the problem.
  2. The confident fake citation. From Chapter 4: AI will happily cite books, studies, and quotes that do not exist. Putting a fabricated citation in a paper is a serious infraction and it's avoidable — verify every source by finding it in your library database or on the actual site.
  3. The paraphrase of a source you never read. AI can summarize an article you didn't read, but if you cite the article (not the AI), you're claiming you know what's in it. Either read it or don't cite it. Reading a linked summary deeply is a middle path — if your instructor allows it and you say so.

Why honesty is also just... better studying

Even if you'd never get caught: using AI to do your work quietly hollows out the skill you came to school for. Remember the push-ups from the intro. Checking sources, writing in your own voice, explaining it back — these are the reps that make you the competent person on the job later. Your future self is the one grading this assignment, six months from now, mid-career.

Try it today

Find the AI policy for one class (syllabus, course site, or ask). Write down where the line is for one assignment you have coming up. Done before lunch — and you've made every later decision easier.
