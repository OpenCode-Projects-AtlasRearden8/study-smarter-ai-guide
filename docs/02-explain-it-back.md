# Chapter 2 — Explain It Back

*Study Smarter with AI*

## The technique, in one breath

After you learn something (from a lecture, a textbook, a video, an AI), close the source and **explain it back to the AI in your own words — out loud or in writing.** Then ask the AI to find the gaps.

That's it. That's the chapter. But let's take this one breath apart, because each piece is doing real work.

## Why it works so well

Two things happen when you explain something back:

1. **You find out what you actually know.** Explaining is like a flashlight — it goes dark exactly where your understanding has holes. That feeling of "wait, why *does* the heart have four chambers and not one?" mid-explanation? That's not failure. That is the *exact* moment learning happens.
2. **You rewrite it in your own language.** Memory stores your words far better than the textbook's words. An explanation in terms of your hometown clinic, your kid's soccer team, or VideoGame lore will outlast any sentence with a semicolon and the phrase "whereas the latter."

This idea has a famous name — the **Feynman Technique**, after the physicist who learned this way — but you don't need to remember the name to use it.

## The prompt that does the work

After finishing a topic, tell the AI:

```
I just studied [topic]. I'm going to explain it back to you
in my own words. Do NOT fill in gaps for me while I talk —
let me finish. Then:

1. Tell me what I explained correctly.
2. Point out anything I glossed over or got wrong, gently.
3. Ask me 2 questions that would catch me if my
   understanding is shallow.
4. If my explanation was solid, ask me a harder
   "what if" question to stretch me.
```

The "do NOT fill in gaps" line matters more than it looks. Helpful AIs love to rescue you, and being rescued interrupts the exact moment of productive struggle where you realize what you don't know. Finish first; gaps later.

## A real-flavored example

Say you studied blood pressure medication basics. Your explain-back might start:

> "Okay so blood pressure meds work in a few ways. Diuretics make you pee more, which lowers the fluid volume, so there's less pressure. ACE inhibitors stop something — I think it's angiotensin? — from tightening the blood vessels..."

You've already spotted it yourself: *something — I think it's angiotensin?* That question mark is the gap. Now the AI's job isn't to lecture; it's to nudge: "You're right that angiotensin tightens vessels. What does the 'ACE' part stand for, and where does the process start?" — and your next explanation is yours, not its.

**A note for healthcare learners:** this pattern is superb for things like giving patient explanations. Explain a medication "to a worried patient," then ask the AI to act as the patient and ask confused follow-up questions. You are rehearsing exactly what you'll do at work. Remember this is practice for *explanations and studying*, never a substitute for your clinical judgment or official protocols.

## Level two: teach the AI

Flip the roles. Ask the AI to play a *student* who is confused, and make **you** the teacher:

```
You're a student studying [topic] and you're confused about
[it]. Ask me questions until you understand it. Don't
understand too easily — push back like a real person.
```

Explaining to a confused "student" is harder and deeper work than explaining to an expert. It exposes every shortcut you've been leaning on.

## Level three: the "explain it to a 10-year-old" test

The classic version: explain the topic with no jargon at all, as if to a bright ten-year-old. If you can't, you've found a spot where the *vocabulary* was holding the *understanding* together. That's gold — that's the thing to go reread.

## Try it today

Pick one thing you "learned" this week — anything, a chapter, a chess opening, a knitting stitch pattern. Close the source. Explain it back to an AI using the prompt above. Then tell a human how it went; explaining it again to a person is level three in disguise.
