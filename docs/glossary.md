# Glossary — Every Term, Zero Jargon

*Study Smarter with AI*

Plain-English definitions of every term this guide uses. Each entry skips the textbook language and tells you what you actually need to know. When a chapter uses a term from this list, it links here like so: [hallucination](#hallucination).

## A

**Active recall** — Trying to remember something *before* looking it up, instead of just reading it again. Pulling from memory is the exercise; looking is just checking your form.

**AI (artificial intelligence)** — In this guide: a computer program you can talk to in normal, everyday language. It learned from enormous amounts of text, which is why it's so good at typing and so important to double-check.

**Academic integrity** — The rules about whose work schoolwork is supposed to represent. Simplest version: your name goes only on work that's actually yours (with help, if allowed and disclosed).

## D

**Deep processing** — Thinking about *what something means* as you study (why it works, how it connects). The opposite of re-reading on repeat. See [levels of processing](#levels-of-processing).

## E

**Elaboration** — Explaining a new idea in your own words and linking it to what you already know. Chapter 2 is a whole chapter of it.

**Escalator, the invisible** — Our name for how "make this sentence better" can slide, one polite prompt at a time, into "write my whole essay." See Chapter 5.

**Explain it back** — Closing the source and teaching the material back to the AI (or a person, or a rubber duck) in your own words. The highest-value technique in this guide.

## F

**Fabricated citation** — A source that doesn't exist. AI sometimes *invents* book titles, studies, quotes, and web pages. Always find the real source yourself before you cite it.

**Feynman Technique** — The fancy name for "explain it back," after physicist Richard Feynman, who famously learned by explaining. You don't need the name to use it.

**Flashcard** — A small question on the front, a small answer on the back. AI works well as a factory for flashcards that you then check.

**Free tier** — The no-cost version of an AI service. Usually has daily use limits. Fully enough for every technique in this guide.

## H

**Hallucination** — When an AI states something false with complete confidence: invented facts, wrong numbers, quotes nobody ever said. Not a glitch you can wait out; a permanent feature to *check against*. Chapter 4 is all about catching them.

## L

**Learning journal** — A simple running log: date, topic, what you missed. Ten seconds after each study session. Over weeks it becomes a personal map of exactly what to review.

**Levels of processing** — The finding that what matters isn't *how long* you studied but *how deeply*: noticing letter shapes is shallow; connecting meaning is deep. Explains why re-reading fails.

**Long-term memory** — The effectively unlimited, long-lasting store in your head. Everything you still know last week lives here. Getting things *into* it is this whole guide's project.

## M

**Massed practice** — Studying one thing in one long block ("cramming"). Same minutes of study, but weaker results than spreading them out. See [spaced repetition](#spaced-repetition).

**Markdown** — A dead-simple way to write notes in plain text with basic symbols for headings, lists, and links. This guide is written in it. Learn it in ten minutes; it's the format that notes, flashcards, and many websites all understand.

## P

**Prompt** — What you type to an AI. A good prompt describes the job *and* the style: "quiz me one question at a time, gently" beats "make a quiz."

**Retrieval practice** — The teacher-word for [active recall](#active-recall). Same idea: memory grows when you pull information *out*, not when you push it (again) *in*.

## Q

**Quiz master prompt** — Our ready-made instruction from Chapter 1 that turns any AI chat into a patient, one-question-at-a-time quizzer built from your own notes.

## R

**Re-reading** — Looking at your notes again. Feels productive, is not. Familiarity is not knowledge — this is the trap this whole guide exists to get you out of.

## S

**Sensory memory** — The sub-second raw buffer of everything your senses just noticed. Most of it evaporates instantly unless you pay attention.

**Short-term (working) memory** — The small whiteboard in your head: roughly four items, 15–30 seconds without rehearsal. Rehearsal moves its contents toward long-term memory; deep processing makes them stay.

**Spaced repetition** — Reviewing material at growing intervals — right about when you'd naturally forget. Closest thing studying has to a cheat code. Chapter 3 gives you the schedule.

**Study partner** — What a well-prompted AI becomes: tireless, never bored, available at 1 a.m. — but *not* a source of verified facts, and not a replacement for your thinking, your syllabus, or your instructors.

## W

**Working memory** — See [short-term memory](#short-term-working-memory).
