# Chapter 7 — Worked Example: One Chapter, Start to Finish

*Study Smarter with AI*

Everything in Chapters 1–6, run on one realistic chunk of study material. Read this the night before your next study session; it's a complete dress rehearsal.

## Our mock textbook chapter

Imagine your course textbook contains this (fictional) chapter. We'll use it because you can follow every AI interaction against a source you can actually check — exactly what Chapter 4 recommends.

> ### Chapter 12: The Science of Memory
>
> **12.1 Three memory stores.** Sensory memory holds raw sensory input for less than a second. Attended information moves into short-term (working) memory, which holds roughly four items for 15–30 seconds without rehearsal. With rehearsal, information encodes into long-term memory, which has effectively unlimited capacity.
>
> **12.2 Encoding matters more than time.** What determines whether something sticks is not how long you looked at it, but *how deeply you processed it*. Shallow processing (noticing letter shapes) produces weak memories; deep processing (connecting meaning, asking why) produces strong ones. The "levels of processing" principle explains why re-reading fails: it is shallow by definition.
>
> **12.2a A note on spacing.** Reviewing material after a delay ("spaced" practice) produces far stronger recall than the same minutes back-to-back ("massed" practice), even when total study time is identical. This is the forgetting-curve principle in action.
>
> **12.3 Retrieval strengthens memory.** Actively recalling information — self-testing — strengthens the memory more than restudying it. Retrieval works best when it is effortful; struggling a little during recall is the signal of a memory being built.
>
> **Key terms:** sensory memory, working memory, long-term memory, encoding, levels of processing, massed vs. spaced practice, retrieval practice.

## Step 1 — The quiz master (Chapter 1)

**You paste the chapter** and use the prompt from Chapter 1. The AI asks:

> **Q1:** A friend says they read their textbook three times and still failed the quiz. Using this chapter, explain why that strategy failed.

**You answer:** "Because re-reading is shallow processing — you're just looking at it again, not doing anything with the meaning."

**AI:** "On target. Here's the detail worth adding: the chapter says re-reading is shallow *by definition*, and shallow processing produces weak memories. Next question — what's the difference between working memory and long-term memory in terms of capacity and duration?"

That's the rhythm. Ten questions, one at a time. Suppose you miss Q6 (you said working memory lasts "about a minute"). The AI corrects: "15–30 seconds without rehearsal — close, but that detail matters on exams." And it remembers to re-ask it before the session ends.

## Step 2 — Explain it back (Chapter 2)

Now you close the chapter and explain:

> "Okay: memory has three stores. Everything hits sensory memory first but dies in a second. What you pay attention to goes into working memory, which is small — like, four things? — and disappears fast unless you rehearse. What decides survival isn't time, it's *depth*: asking why stuff connects beats staring at it. Real memory gets built by pulling things back OUT — self-testing — and the effort is the point."

Then the critique prompt from Chapter 2. The AI replies:

> "Strong. Two things: (1) 'four things' is right — this chapter says about four items. (2) You called effort during recall 'the point' — correct, connect that explicitly to why quizzing beats re-reading. My check: why does spaced review work better, in your own words?"

You nailed it; you found it felt *harder* than reading. That difficulty is the mechanism, not a problem.

## Step 3 — Make flashcards (Chapter 1, output for Chapter 3)

**You ask for 15 flashcards in FRONT/BACK format.** The AI returns a set including:

```
FRONT: Why does re-reading feel productive but fail?
BACK: It's shallow processing — familiarity, not retrieval.
```

**Now you check them against the chapter, line by line.** Chapter 4 in action. Suppose one card says:

```
FRONT: How long does sensory memory last?
BACK: Up to 3 seconds.
```

Your chapter says *less than a second*. Caught. You fix the card — and you'll remember the fix precisely *because* you caught it yourself (effortful recall works for corrections too).

## Step 4 — Schedule it (Chapter 3)

You paste the flashcards and ask for a spaced plan. You get a 10-minutes-or-less checklist, and put four calendar events in: day 0 (today, done), day 2, day 7, day 21 — "Review Ch. 12 + AI quiz." On day 7 you tell the AI what you missed on day 2 and it weights the quiz toward your misses.

## Step 5 — Verify and cite honestly (Chapters 4 and 5)

Two real-life checks:

- **Verify by testing:** our chapter is fictional, so a source-check would fail — which is the built-in lesson. In real study, your chapter is in your licensed textbook: that's your verification source for every quiz answer.
- **Cite honestly:** on that week's reflection assignment, you write: "I used an AI chat to quiz myself on Chapter 12 before writing this." One sentence, fully green-light, matches Chapter 5.

## What just happened, in numbers

Roughly 45 minutes total across five small steps — versus (be honest) 90 minutes of re-reading with a highlighted marker and weak recall to show for it. Every technique used is free, and every one transfers to any subject: pharmacology, algebra, chess openings, knitting.
